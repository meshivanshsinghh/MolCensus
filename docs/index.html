MolCensus

A benchmark of tools that turn chemical drawings in research papers into machine-readable molecules, tested on whole papers instead of single clean images.

Chemistry papers show molecules as drawings. To search or compare them, a program has to turn each drawing back into a molecule. Tools that do this are called OCSR tools, for optical chemical structure recognition. They are usually tested on single drawings already cut out of patents, where the best of them are right more than 90% of the time. MolCensus tests them on whole papers instead. It renders every page, finds each drawing, reads it, and compares the result with a list of every molecule the paper reports.

The test set is six medicinal chemistry papers from a Northeastern University course (AAI 6630, fall 2026), with 220 distinct molecules, plus public image sets and 12 papers from the BioVista benchmark. The six papers and their answer key are private, so this page reports totals per paper and never shows a molecule, a drawing or a compound name.

Five findings

  1. Installation checks catch silent failures. Four failures ran to the end without an error and were caught only by comparing output with known answers.
  2. Where molecules are lost. 138 of the 146 molecules the crop readers missed exist only as rows of R-group tables, so reading one drawing at a time can reach at most 34% to 37% recall on these papers.
  3. BioMiner's R-group step. Counting every whole structure BioMiner reads gives 34.1% recall at 78.9% precision on the course papers, and its table step found 12 molecules no crop reader found.
  4. Reader agreement vs confidence. Molecules on which three readers agree are right more often than MolScribe's most confident readings, but six papers cannot yet show the difference is real.
  5. Public benchmarks vs real papers. Public image sets rank the readers differently from real papers, and a scoring choice about tautomers moves one public score by nine points.

1 Installation checks catch silent failures

The four failures below each ran to the end without an error message, and each was caught only because a check compared the output with a known answer.

The first run on a Mac scored MolScribe at 34.8% on the ACS journal images, against 71.9% in its paper. MolScribe returned no molecule for any drawing that uses an abbreviation such as Me or Ph. The step that expands an abbreviation reads bond orders from bonds it has already deleted, which happens to fail on macOS, and MolScribe hides the error. A three-line patch brought ACS accuracy to 73.1%.

The first GPU run on AWS gave MolScribe and MolSight a score of zero on every public image set. The machine image put its own copy of cuDNN, NVIDIA's library of neural-network routines, on the library search path. PyTorch loaded half of its cuDNN from its own package and half from that folder, and every convolution failed. The usual test, torch.cuda.is_available(), still reported a working GPU. Setup now runs one real convolution in each Python environment. After the fix, 200 USPTO patent images gave MolScribe 92.9% and MolSight 91.4%, against 92.6% and 91.2% in their papers.

MinerU, the PDF layout tool inside BioMiner, crashed on a missing model file and still exited with status 0, the code for success. Its setup check now passes only if MinerU actually wrote its text output.

BioMiner sends parts of each paper to a large language model (LLM). It tries each call once and carries on with an empty answer if the call fails, so a rate-limited run looks normal. In the first run, a Google AI Studio key that was meant to be paid turned out to be held to free-tier limits of 5 requests a minute and 20 a day. Eight calls were answered and 21 were refused for quota. Because the wrapper counts failed calls, the smoke test (one short paper run before the real stages) failed at its 45-minute limit, and the BioMiner stages were skipped instead of being scored on empty answers. The second run reached Gemini through Google Cloud Vertex AI, signed in from the AWS instance without any stored key: 330 calls were answered, 4 were retried after a short rate limit, and none were lost.

These checks cover the failures we hit. A fault that still produces believable numbers on the public sets would get through.

2 Where molecules are lost

On the course papers the crop readers are close to the most they can find one drawing at a time: 138 of the 146 molecules they missed exist only as rows of R-group tables.

An R-group table is a drawing of a shared core with labelled open positions (R1, R2), plus a table that says what sits at each position for each compound. The compound itself is never drawn. Three readers, MolScribe, MolSight and MolGlyph, read the same 237 drawings that the MolDetV2 detector cut out of the six papers. Scored by InChIKey (a fixed-length code computed from a molecule, so that two ways of writing the same molecule match), they found 73 of the 219 distinct molecules between them, or 33.3%.

Each of the 146 missed molecules was sorted into one cause. Automatic tests came first: a second detection pass at a much lower threshold, an exact substructure test of every reading with open positions against each missed molecule, and a similarity test for near misses. The remaining molecules went to two AI judges, Claude and Gemini 3.5 Flash, who saw the same page images and instructions. Before their answers counted, both were tested blind on the 213 molecules whose answer was already known. Claude got 206 right (96.7%) and Gemini 198 (93.0%). Their agreement over all 219 molecules was a Cohen's kappa of 0.904; kappa measures agreement beyond chance, with 1 for perfect agreement and 0 for chance. A judge label was used only when both judges gave it.

No missed molecule turned out to be drawn in full but missed by the detector, and none was absent from the figures. The two reading losses are both in paper 16, whose large ring molecules are drawn under a colored heatmap. One rule withdrew an automatic label when both judges contradicted it; it fired five times. Under the strict rule, without that withdrawal, the totals are 142 table losses, 3 reading losses and 1 unresolved.

So between 75 and 81 of the 219 molecules are drawn in full somewhere in the papers, a recall ceiling of 34.2% to 37.0% for any reader that works one drawing at a time. Better reading could add at most 2 to 8 molecules. Finding more needs a tool that reads tables.

The automatic table test treats every symbol a reader could not expand as an open position, so a drawn molecule with an unusual abbreviation can pass for a table compound; the judges caught four such cases. The judges are not independent of the analysis: Claude wrote the evidence code. Gemini's answers vary from run to run, and the cached answers are the ones used. The second detection pass found no missed drawing, which is evidence, not proof, that detection loses nothing.

3 BioMiner's R-group step

BioMiner ran with Gemini 3.5 Flash on Google Cloud Vertex AI, not with its own fine-tuned model, BioMiner-Instruct, and on 12 BioVista papers rather than the 450 its authors used.

BioMiner (Yan et al., 2026) processes a whole PDF. MinerU finds the page layout, MolDetV2 finds the drawings, MolGlyph reads them, and a language model ties each structure to its compound label and fills in R-group tables.

BioMiner fills in R-group tables, so it finds molecules that no crop reader can. Counting every whole structure it reads gives the best balance of recall and precision on the course papers.

What counts as a BioMiner prediction matters, and three rules are reported. Linked structures are what BioMiner itself keeps: the structures its language model tied to a compound label. On the course papers that is 58 structures. All extracted structures adds every structure MolGlyph read inside BioMiner, linked or not (115 more). The headline rule drops from those the 78 pieces with open positions (written with * atoms), which are table scaffolds and substituent fragments rather than molecules. Matching here is by canonical isomeric SMILES (RDKit's standard spelling of a molecule, including its stereochemistry marks), over 220 distinct molecules.

Under the headline rule BioMiner finds 75 of 220 molecules (34.1%) with 78.9% of its predictions correct. The best crop reader, MolGlyph, finds 61 at 37.4% precision. Obaidullah's BioMiner run on the same six papers reached 34.5% recall at 72.4% precision (Obaidullah, 2026). His figures imply about 105 predictions and match ours when every structure read is counted, rather than the linked ones alone; his model has not been confirmed and is probably BioMiner-Instruct. Dropping the pieces with open positions also drops two correct molecules from paper 19 whose answer key itself writes a generic group as an open position, which is why the headline rule finds 75 molecules and the all-extracted rule 77.

BioMiner found 12 molecules that none of the three crop readers found, 9 in paper 4 and 3 in paper 19. All 12 came from filling in R-group tables. That is BioMiner's own contribution over a crop reader. Recall on those two papers is still low, 38.3% and 25.0% at best, because most of their table compounds were not filled in.

BioVista: finding a structure vs linking it to a label

BioVista (Yan et al., 2026) is BioMiner's own benchmark of papers with every structure labelled. On 12 of its papers, the structures MolGlyph read inside BioMiner were scored by type, once as linked to a compound label and once counting every whole structure read. For full structures, counting what MolGlyph read but Gemini did not link closes most of the gap to the published result: mean recall rises from 36.1% to 59.4%, against 65.4%. Every structure BioMiner links as a full structure is one of these readings, so no linking step could do better than this count with the same detector and reader. For R-group tables almost nothing changes (28.3% to 28.6%, against 33.9%). Those molecules exist only after the table is filled in, which is itself a step done by the language model. Precision over all structures stays near half (52.2% linked, 53.7% counting everything read), against 61.5% published. On 145 BioVista papers, Obaidullah's BioMiner run reached 58.7% recall at 62.7% precision, close to the published result (Obaidullah, 2026), and he found 74% recall for fully drawn compounds against 40% for table-defined ones.

These are 12 papers, not 450, and they were not chosen to match BioVista's test set. BioMiner's own evaluator, run on the same 12 papers with its matching rule, gives linked means within half a point of ours, so the matching rule does not explain the gap. A run with BioMiner-Instruct on the same papers would separate the model's share from the sample's.

4 Reader agreement vs confidence

When all three readers write the same molecule for a drawing, it is right more often than MolScribe's most confident readings, but six papers are not enough to show that the difference is real.

A reader's confidence is its own estimate that a reading is right; of the three readers only MolScribe reports one. All three readers agreed on 59 of the 237 drawings. Of the distinct molecules from those drawings, 71.4% were correct (40 of 56). MolScribe's 59 most confident drawings gave 59.6%. With at least two readers agreeing (119 drawings) the figure was 56.0%, against 45.4% for MolScribe's top 119.

Molecules from the same paper succeed or fail together, so the test resamples whole papers rather than molecules. Over 10,000 draws of the six papers with replacement, using the same draws for both selections, the gap in precision for the 59-drawing comparison is +11.8 points, with a 95% interval from −1.0 to +15.5; 3.8% of the draws favor confidence or tie. For the 119-drawing comparison it is +10.6 points (−7.7 to +19.6). Agreement leads in 3 of the 4 papers where both selections have predictions.

Obaidullah's benchmark of six readers on five public image sets shows why agreement could help (Obaidullah, 2026): the best reader changes with the source of the images. OCSRGlyph was best on USPTO patents and on CLEF, and fourth of six on MolRecBench-Wild journal images, where MolGlyph led. Readers that fail on different images are what an agreement filter needs. The VERDICT preprint reports the same direction on real documents (Guan et al., 2026).

A tautomer is one of several forms of a molecule that differ only in where a hydrogen atom sits and that turn into each other easily. The standard InChIKey treats them as one molecule; canonical SMILES does not. On the UOB set MolScribe scores 95.9% by InChIKey and 87.3% by canonical SMILES. Of the 489 images that match only by InChIKey, 473 differ only by tautomer, 11 by stereo marks, 1 by charge and 4 in some other way, so the drawing and the label probably show different tautomers. Under canonical SMILES MolScribe's interval includes its published 87.9%.

The same rule reproduces our teammates' numbers. On the same 5,740 UOB images, Obaidullah measured 87.3% for MolScribe and 87.0% for MolGlyph (Obaidullah, 2026); we measure 87.3% and 87.0%. On USPTO and CLEF his image sets are larger than ours (5,704 and 977 crops against 5,633 and 904), and the numbers differ by up to seven points. On BioVista, Syeda's sample of 100 images gave MolScribe 48% and MolGlyph 67% (Syeda, 2026). Our MolScribe score on all 8,860 images, 45.2%, is inside the 95% interval of her sample (38.5 to 57.7%). Our MolGlyph score, 77.7%, is above hers (57.3 to 75.4%), and the reason has not been found.

The ranking flips on real papers

On USPTO patent images MolScribe is the best of the three readers (93.3%) and MolGlyph the worst (74.1%). On the course papers the order reverses: MolGlyph finds the most molecules (29.2%) and MolScribe the fewest (23.3%). Paired molecule by molecule, MolGlyph found 16 molecules that MolScribe missed and MolScribe found 3 that MolGlyph missed (exact McNemar test, p = 0.0044). Obaidullah's six-reader table shows the same pattern: MolGlyph trails on USPTO but leads on MolRecBench-Wild journal images.

Public crops are clean, single, already-cut drawings. They test reading, not finding or table work, and our image sets are not identical to the published ones or to our teammates'.

Methods

The pipeline renders each PDF page at 300 dpi with PyMuPDF, finds molecule drawings with MolDetV2 (a YOLO detector, confidence 0.4), and passes each cut-out drawing, called a crop, to a reader that writes it as a SMILES string. SMILES is a line of text that spells out a molecule's atoms and bonds. RDKit turns each string into a molecule or rejects it as invalid. BioMiner runs separately on whole PDFs.

Scoring is per paper. Crops do not line up one to one with the answer key, so each paper's distinct predicted molecules are compared with its distinct reference molecules as two sets. Recall is the share of reference molecules found; precision is the share of distinct valid predictions that are reference molecules. Two matching rules are used: the full InChIKey, and the canonical isomeric SMILES. Each rate gets a 95% Wilson interval, which treats molecules as independent, and pooled rates also get a paper bootstrap interval, which resamples the six papers 2,000 times and is wider because molecules from one paper succeed or fail together. Paired comparisons between two tools use the exact McNemar test, a binomial test on the molecules only one of the two found.

The public image sets are USPTO, UOB, CLEF and ACS from MolScribe's evaluation archive, and the 8,861 BioVista crops, scored one image at a time. The recall-ceiling labels and the BioMiner prediction rules are described in the findings above. Details, file formats and every threshold are in docs/methods.md.

Limitations

Six papers is a small sample. Two of them hold 1 and 4 molecules, so their per-paper scores swing widely, and the paper bootstrap intervals are wide because the papers differ so much. Papers 4 and 19 hold 164 of the 219 molecules and dominate every pooled number.

The answer key lists each paper's molecules without saying where they appear, and nobody checked it against the papers for this project. Five of its entries are themselves written with open positions.

BioMiner ran with Gemini 3.5 Flash, not BioMiner-Instruct, and on 12 BioVista papers instead of 450. Its BioVista numbers say how this setup does on those papers, not how BioMiner does in general.

The recall-ceiling labels rest on automatic tests and on two AI judges, one of which is the assistant that wrote the analysis code. No label was set by hand.

Every stage ran once. The only repeat is MolScribe on the Mac CPU and on the GPU, which gave the same SMILES. There is no ground truth for detection boxes, so detection is checked only indirectly.
