The problem

Clean crops are not real papers

Tools that read chemical drawings, called OCSR tools for optical chemical structure recognition, turn each drawing into SMILES, a line of text that spells out a molecule's atoms and bonds. They are usually tested on single drawings already cut out of patents, where the best of them are right more than nine times in ten.

Medicinal chemistry papers are harder. Molecules sit inside reaction schemes and figures, and a whole series of compounds is often printed once, as a shared core with a table of substituents. MolCensus measures what that does to the tools, paper by paper, against a list of every molecule each paper reports.

The six course papers and their answer key are private, so this page reports totals per paper and never shows a molecule, a drawing or a compound name. The drawing on the right is made up.

How it works

Two pipelines, one answer key

Each paper is rendered, its drawings are found and read, and the results are compared with a list of every molecule the paper reports. BioMiner runs the same idea end to end, with a language model that links structures to labels.

Scoring

Drawings do not line up one to one with the answer key, so each paper's distinct predicted molecules are compared with its distinct reference molecules as two sets. Recall is the share of reference molecules found; precision is the share of predictions that are right.

Uncertainty

Every rate gets a 95% Wilson interval. Pooled rates also get a paper bootstrap, which resamples the six papers and is wider, because molecules from one paper succeed or fail together.

Data

Six private course papers with 220 molecules; public crops from USPTO, UOB, CLEF and ACS; 8,861 BioVista crops and 12 BioVista papers. Matching uses InChIKey and canonical SMILES from RDKit.

Finding 2

Almost every missed molecule lives in an R-group table

138 of the 146 molecules the crop readers missed are never drawn as a whole. They exist only as rows of a table under a shared core.

Three crop readers, MolScribe, MolSight and MolGlyph, read the same 237 drawings that the MolDetV2 detector cut out of the six papers. Matched by InChIKey (a fixed-length code computed from a molecule, so two spellings of the same molecule match), they found 73 of the 219 molecules between them.

Every one of the 146 misses was then traced to a cause. Automatic tests went first: a second detection pass at a much lower threshold, an exact substructure test of every reading with open positions, and a similarity test for near misses. Two AI judges, Claude and Gemini 3.5 Flash, looked at the rest using the page images. Their answers counted only when both agreed, and only after both passed a blind test on molecules with known answers.

No missed molecule turned out to be a full drawing the detector skipped, and none was missing from the figures. The two reading losses are large ring molecules in paper 16, drawn under a colored heatmap. Between 75 and 81 of the 219 molecules are drawn in full anywhere in the papers. Better reading could add at most 2 to 8 molecules; the rest need a tool that reads tables. One rule withdrew an automatic label when both judges contradicted it. Without that rule the totals are 142 table losses, 3 reading losses and 1 unresolved.

CaveatThe automatic table test treats any symbol a reader could not expand as an open position, so an odd abbreviation can pass for a table compound; the judges caught four such cases. Claude also wrote the evidence code, so the judges are not fully independent of the analysis.
Finding 3

BioMiner's table step finds what crop readers cannot

Counting every whole structure BioMiner reads gives 34.1% recall at 78.9% precision on the course papers, the best balance of any tool here.

BioMiner ran with Gemini 3.5 Flash on Google Cloud Vertex AI, not with its own fine-tuned model, BioMiner-Instruct, and on 12 BioVista papers rather than the 450 its authors used.

BioMiner (Yan et al., 2026) works on the whole PDF. MinerU finds the layout, MolDetV2 the drawings and MolGlyph reads them, then a language model ties each structure to its compound label and fills in the tables. What counts as a BioMiner prediction turned out to matter. BioMiner itself keeps only the 58 structures it linked to a label. Its reader found 115 more, and 78 of those are table scaffolds and substituent pieces rather than molecules. The headline counts every whole structure, linked or not.

On BioVista, most of the gap is linking

BioVista (Yan et al., 2026) is BioMiner's own benchmark, with every structure in each paper labelled. On 12 of its papers I scored the structures by type twice: once as BioMiner links them to compound labels, and once counting every whole structure its reader found. For full structures, counting what MolGlyph read but Gemini did not link lifts recall from 36.1% to 59.4%, close to the published 65.4%. Every linked full structure is one of these readings, so no linker could do better with the same detector and reader. For R-group tables almost nothing changes, because those compounds exist only after the language model fills in the table.

Finding 4

Agreement between readers beats confidence, so far

When all three readers write the same molecule for a drawing, it is right more often than MolScribe's most confident readings. Six papers are not yet enough to show the gap is real.

A reader's confidence is its own estimate that a reading is right; of the three, only MolScribe reports one. All three readers wrote the same molecule for 59 of the 237 drawings, and 71.4% of those molecules were correct. MolScribe's 59 most confident drawings gave 59.6%. With two readers agreeing the figures are 56.0% and 45.4%.

Molecules from the same paper succeed or fail together, so the test resamples whole papers: 10,000 draws of the six papers, the same draws for both selections. Agreement leads in 3 of the 4 papers where both selections have predictions, but both intervals cross zero.

Finding 5

Public benchmarks do not predict real papers

Public image sets rank the readers differently from real papers, and the rule for matching molecules can move a public score by nine points.

On USPTO patent images MolScribe is the best of the three readers and MolGlyph the worst. On the course papers the order reverses: MolGlyph finds the most molecules and MolScribe the fewest. Paired molecule by molecule, MolGlyph found 16 that MolScribe missed and MolScribe found 3 that MolGlyph missed (exact McNemar test, p = 0.0044). Obaidullah's six-reader table shows the same pattern, with MolGlyph trailing on USPTO and leading on journal images.

One scoring choice moves a public score by nine points

A tautomer is one of several forms of a molecule that differ only in where a hydrogen atom sits. The standard InChIKey treats them as one molecule; canonical SMILES does not. On UOB, MolScribe scores 95.9% by InChIKey and 87.3% by canonical SMILES. Of the 489 images that match only by InChIKey, 473 differ only by tautomer, so the drawings and the labels probably show different forms. Under canonical SMILES the published 87.9% falls inside our interval.

Our numbers match the team's

On the same 5,740 UOB images, Obaidullah measured 87.3% for MolScribe and 87.0% for MolGlyph (Obaidullah, 2026); we measure 87.3% and 87.0%. On USPTO and CLEF his image sets are larger than ours, and the numbers differ by up to seven points. On BioVista, Syeda's 100-image sample gave MolScribe 48% and MolGlyph 67% (Syeda, 2026). Our MolScribe score on all 8,860 images, 45.2%, falls inside her interval (38.5 to 57.7%). Our MolGlyph score, 77.7%, is above hers (57.3 to 75.4%), and the reason is not known yet.

How it got done

Eighteen days, two GPU runs

  1. 19 Oct

    First full run on a laptop

    The pipeline ran end to end on a MacBook CPU. The public check caught a MolScribe bug on macOS, and a patch fixed it that night.

  2. 20 Oct

    First results

    MolScribe found 51 of 219 course molecules, with intervals and a breakdown of what happened to each miss.

  3. 29 Oct

    More readers, more data

    MolSight and MolGlyph joined in their own environments. BioVista labels and the AWS setup scripts went in.

  4. 3 Nov

    Getting a GPU

    At least 98 attempts to start a GPU instance in us-east-2 were refused for lack of capacity before a g5.2xlarge came up. Its first run scored two readers at zero, and the cuDNN fix followed.

  5. 4 Nov

    The overnight run

    Three readers read 21,643 public images in 373 minutes. BioMiner was stopped by the free-tier Gemini quota.

  6. 4 Nov

    Keys out

    Gemini moved to Vertex AI. The instance now signs in to Google through workload identity federation, with no key stored on it.

  7. 5 Nov

    BioMiner end to end

    Six course papers and 12 BioVista papers in about 70 minutes on one L4 GPU: 330 Gemini calls, none lost.

  8. 5 Nov

    Tracing the misses

    Every missed molecule was sorted into a cause, with automatic tests and two AI judges.

  9. 5 Nov

    Published

    Code and report went public. All cloud resources were then deleted.

Limits

What these results cannot tell you

Small sample

Six papers, two of them with 1 and 4 molecules. Papers 4 and 19 hold 164 of the 219 molecules and dominate every pooled number.

Unchecked answer key

The key lists each paper's molecules without saying where they appear, and it was not audited for this project. Five entries are themselves written with open positions.

Not BioMiner's own model

BioMiner ran with Gemini 3.5 Flash instead of BioMiner-Instruct, on 12 BioVista papers instead of 450.

AI judges

The recall-ceiling labels rest on automatic tests and two AI judges, one of which wrote the analysis code. No label was set by hand.

One run each

Every stage ran once. MolScribe on the laptop and on the GPU gave identical SMILES, the only repeat. Detection has no ground-truth boxes.
